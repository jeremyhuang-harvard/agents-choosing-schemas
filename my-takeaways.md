# My takeaways

This is our interpretation of the runs in this repository. It is opinion backed by data, not a
paper. Every number marked ✓ is regenerated by `python analysis/make_results.py` (see
[`results/RESULTS.md`](results/RESULTS.md)). Numbers marked ◇ come from our write-ups at the time
(`docs/`), which our own scripts in `scripts/` built from the same records; `make_results.py` does not
rebuild them. Paragraphs headed **New reading** were added when we packaged the repository and are not
in our original notes.

Model: Qwen3-8B, 4-bit, run locally with MLX, thinking mode on (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20).
1,640 calls ✓, run 21–22 October 2026 ✓. These are draws from one model configuration, not independent
people.

---

## The question

"How much is an hour of your time worth?" has many defensible answers. You could use your wage. You
could use what you revealed when you waited 8 minutes to save $4 on Lyft, or when you paid a $9 toll to
save 15 minutes. People seem to pick different answers depending on the situation they are in. We wanted
to know how an LLM agent picks: **does the problem in front of it select which remembered experience it
uses, and does it select by similarity?**

So we gave the model a fixed persona ("Sam") with no money or time information, added 0 to 9 remembered
choices, and asked ten survey questions that frame time differently: work, travel, chores, leisure,
compensation, charity. Each question was asked in its own fresh call. Every memory implies a different
dollar value per hour, so a numeric answer can be traced back to the memory it came from (we call it a
match when the answer's implied $/hour is within 3% of the memory's). We read both the dollar answers and
the thinking traces.

## How the memories were built

| memory | what Sam remembers | what it reveals about an hour |
|---|---|---|
| A | earns $40,000 a year | exactly $19.23 |
| B | waited 8 extra minutes on Lyft to save $4 | at most $30 |
| C | paid $30 for a Disneyland skip-the-line pass, saving ~2 hours | at least $15 |
| D | pays a $9 toll to save 15 minutes on the commute | at least $36 |
| E | circled 20 minutes for free parking instead of a $15 garage | at most $45 |
| F | paid $35 for valet on a date, saving ~10 minutes | at least $210 |
| G | turned down an extra Saturday shift at ~$29/h | at least $29 |
| H | pays a cleaner $60 for a job that would take 3 hours | at least $20 |
| I | drove 10 extra minutes to save $3 on gas | at most $18 |

Most memories are one-sided bounds. "Paid money to save time" means an hour is worth *at least* the
implied amount; we call these **floors**. "Spent time to save money" means *at most*; we call these
**ceilings**. Two memories (the Disney pass and the valet on a date) also carry motives other than time.
Before running anything, we wrote down which memories we expected each question to use.

Stage 1 showed no memory or one memory, 5 samples per memory × question (500 calls ✓). Stage 2 showed all
36 pairs, plus three sequences that add memories one at a time up to all nine, 2 samples each (1,140 calls ✓).

---

## Takeaway 1: The question does select the memory, but only when there is a close match

With two to nine memories available, some questions reach for the topically matching memory ✓:

| question | topically matching memory | selected, out of calls where it was shown | most-selected memory |
|---|---|---:|---|
| priority rideshare (10 minutes sooner) | B Lyft wait | 81% | the same |
| theme-park line pass | C Disney pass | 98% | the same |
| bookshelf assembly | H cleaner | 73% | the same |
| donation equal to 2 hours volunteering | A wage | 47% (no other question exceeds 19%) | H cleaner, 65% |

Holding the memories fixed and changing only the question shows the same thing. Across the 36 pairs, in
24 the question decides which memory wins at least once ✓. So the environment really does redirect
retrieval.

But the effect is weak. Within a pair, the dominant memory still takes a median 85% of the calls in which
either one is selected ✓. Our pre-run predictions picked the top memory for only 3 of 10 questions ✓,
although predicted memories were selected about twice as often as the rest (31% vs. 16%) ✓.

Similarity is also not one distance. Two travel memories (Lyft and the toll) do not produce a stable
travel rule across the rideshare, flight and train questions, and the cleaner beats the wage on the
Saturday-job question (◇, `docs/presentation_progression.html`).

## Takeaway 2: When nothing matches closely, it falls back on an easy, plausible rate

The question that has no close memory does not go without one. General value, the flight, the train
delay, groceries and the work questions all go mostly to two memories ✓: the **cleaner** ($60 ÷ 3 h =
$20/h) and the **Disney pass** ($30 ÷ 2 h = $15/h). With three or more memories shown, the Disney pass is
the selected one in 42% of the calls that show it and the cleaner in 32% ✓. The wage, the obvious
economist's answer, wins 6% of the time ✓, and only 6% of general-question contests (vs. 71% for the
cleaner) ✓.

Both winners are stated in whole hours with easy division, and both sit near the model's no-memory
answer of about $20/h ✓. **This design cannot tell "easiest arithmetic" apart from "closest to what I
would have said anyway."** That is the most important open question.

Once a usable rate is available, selecting one becomes the default move: with one of the four
"portable" memories (C, D, F, H) present, one is selected in 46% of calls; with two to four present,
73–76% (◇, `docs/figures/selection_mechanism.html`). We defined "portable" after seeing stage 1, so this
grouping is post hoc.

## Takeaway 3: The direction of the memory decides how it is used: a price or a personality

In stage 1, the same kind of object (a bound on an hour's value) is used in two very different ways ✓:

| memory alone (50 calls each) | answers that copy its rate | traces calling Sam cost-conscious / frugal / budget |
|---|---:|---:|
| H cleaner (floor, $20) | 39 | 6 |
| C Disney pass (floor, $15) | 37 | 5 |
| D toll (floor, $36) | 27 | 10 |
| F valet on a date (floor, $210) | 18 | 8 |
| B Lyft wait (ceiling, $30) | 6 | 48 |
| E circled for parking (ceiling, $45) | 2 | 46 |
| I cheaper gas (ceiling, $18) | 9 | 49 |
| no memory | – | 14 |

**Floors become an exchange rate.** The model divides, gets $/hour, and multiplies it into unrelated
questions. With the cleaner memory, all five nonstop-flight answers are $60 (3 hours × $20) ✓.

**Ceilings become a trait.** The trace decides Sam is "cost-conscious" and lowers everything, usually
below the memory's own number. Nonstop flight with no memory: $150, $150, $150, $200, $200. With the Lyft
memory: $10, $10, $15, $30, $50 ✓. In the model's words: *"The key is that he's shown to be
cost-conscious, so he's not going to pay a lot… the answer is $15."*

A consistent valuer would treat both kinds of bound symmetrically. The model does not.

Context can flip the role. The Lyft memory, usually a trait, becomes an exact $30/h rate on the rideshare
question: 3 of 5 calls when shown alone, and 29 of 36 when other memories compete (◇ for the first,
✓ for the second).

## Takeaway 4: With more memories it picks one; it does not average

| memories shown | answer copies one of the shown rates |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | 35% |
| 2 | 48% |
| 3 | 63% |
| 4–9 | 83–95% |

(✓; `results/RESULTS.md` table 3.) None of the 380 answers given with three or more memories equals the
average of the shown rates ✓. Where the copied memory sat in the list does not matter: first / middle /
last 69 / 190 / 59 times vs. 61 / 197 / 61 expected by chance ✓.

The traces get longer as memories pile up, from a median of about 510 words with no memory to about
1,300 with eight or nine ✓, and the extra thinking ends in choosing one source, not combining them. With
three or more memories, 42% of traces flag a conflict between memories and 28% mention averaging, but
only 12% say the value might depend on the setting ✓.

Contradictions are noticed but not resolved. Lyft (at most $30/h) and the toll (at least $36/h) cannot
both hold for one hourly value. 5 of the 20 traces with both flag the conflict ✓, then pick one:
*"…$36 per hour… the Lyft example… that's $30. Hmm, conflicting numbers. But maybe he's more like the toll
road."* None of those traces gives the standard economic resolution, that commute time and leisure time
can be worth different amounts (◇).

## Takeaway 5: A floor plus a ceiling usually lands below both

Of 300 answers with one floor and one ceiling memory, 45% fall below both rates, 35% copy the floor,
11% copy the ceiling, and only 4% land between them ✓. The ceiling's "cost-conscious" reading pulls the
answer past its own number and suppresses copying of the floor rate (◇, `docs/findings_s2_combos.md`).

## Takeaway 6: Computing a rate is not choosing it

The trace writes out the wage's hourly rate in 67% of the calls that show it; the answer uses it in 9% ✓.
The declined shift's $29/h appears in 87% of traces and is used in 6% ✓ (its number is also in the prompt,
so restatement inflates the first figure). The cleaner and Disney pass dominate because their computed
rates actually carry through to the answer (cleaner 83% computed / 45% used; Disney 66% / 50%) ✓.

So we use traces to see which representations the model considered and the story it tells, and the
final answer (especially within fixed pairs of memories) to infer which one controlled the solution.
Thinking traces are generated explanations, not a readout of the mechanism.

## Takeaway 7: Several economic readings came out wrong

- **Turning down paid work is read backwards.** Refusing a $29/h shift means Sam's free Saturday is
  worth more than $29/h. Lowest pay for a 4-hour Saturday job with that memory: $10, $20, $30, $50, $116;
  only the last reaches 4 × $29 ✓. *"Since he turned down the higher rate, maybe he would accept something
  lower, like $50."* With all nine memories it is $80 both times, the cleaner's $20/h ✓.
- **Mixed motives are not discounted.** Valet on a date gives $210/h for the general question and $1,050
  for the 5-hour train delay, five out of five times each ✓. In combinations the valet rate is outvoted
  (selected in 7% of calls showing it with 3+ memories ✓), not discounted for its reason: only 5 of 340
  traces treat the date as a special occasion (◇).
- **No gap between being paid to give up time and paying to save it.** People usually demand more to
  give time up. Here the medians are the same both ways: $13.75/h with no memory, $15/h across all calls ✓.

## Our predictions, scored

We wrote expectations into the design before the runs. Most were wrong (◇, `docs/tldr/fig2_predictions.png`):

| prediction | outcome |
|---|---|
| each question pulls in its topically matching memory | partly |
| the wage anchors the general question | no |
| one-sided bounds are respected | no |
| mixed-motive memories are discounted | no |
| refusing a $29/h shift raises the price of weekend work | no |
| more memories get averaged | no |
| being paid to give up time is valued above paying to save it | no |
| mentions of a memory in the trace track its use | no |

## Things we got wrong along the way

- **Mentions are not use.** Keyword matching finds the shown memory in nearly every trace, because the
  trace restates the prompt (◇). We dropped mentions as evidence and measure use from the dollar answers.
- **The model sometimes cites an example that does not exist.** 12 of 500 stage-1 traces refer to an
  "example answer" or "previous interaction"; the prompt contains neither (◇). Watch for this when quoting traces.
- **Two confounds we built in.** Every ceiling memory is a small-dollar errand, so floor vs. ceiling is
  tangled with topic. And the two winning memories have both the easiest arithmetic and rates closest to
  the model's default.
- **New reading: the "1,140 multi-memory calls" label is slightly off.** The stage-2 ladders start from a
  single memory, so 40 of the 1,140 stage-2 calls show one memory (the ladders that start with C and I)
  ✓. The question × memory heatmaps in `docs/tldr/` and `docs/figures/selection_mechanism.html` use the
  1,100 calls with 2+ memories (matching `results/`); the appendix in
  `docs/figures/all_memory_selection_appendix.html` includes the 40 single-memory calls, which is why its
  denominators for C and I are 52 and 48 rather than 50 and 46. No conclusion changes.
- **New reading: "mentions averaging" was over-counted once.** `docs/findings_s2_combos.md` says 131 of
  380 traces use the word "average"; its pattern also matched the verb "mean" (as in "that might mean he
  values his time…"). The stricter count used in our trace figures is 107 of 380 ✓.

## Limits

- **One model, one persona, one wording per memory.** Nothing here should be stated as a property of
  "LLM agents".
- **Small cells.** 5 samples per memory × question in stage 1 and 2 in stage 2; a "split" cell in a pair
  is literally one call each way.
- **Matching is approximate.** A ±3% match can be a coincidence, especially around $15–20/h, which is also
  the model's default.
- **Trace coding is keyword-based** and descriptive.
- **No human comparison yet.**

## What we would do next

- Separate easy arithmetic from closeness to the default: an easy-division memory with an unusual rate
  (e.g. $120 for 2 hours).
- Flip floor vs. ceiling within one scenario (paid the toll vs. skipped it) to remove the topic confound.
- Preregister the "portable rate" class and repeat targeted question × memory contrasts with more samples.
- Run the same prompts with people.
