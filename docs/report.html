Which memories does an LLM use to value its time?

A thinking model answers “what is an hour of your time worth?” from a persona plus remembered choices, each of which implies a different dollar value. We vary which memories it sees and ask ten differently framed questions, each in its own call, and read both the dollar answers and the thinking traces.

Qwen3-8B (4-bit, MLX), thinking on · 1,640 calls · repo agentic-norms-learning · draws from one model configuration, not independent people.

Summary
  1. “Paid to save time” memories become an exchange rate. The model divides, gets $/hour, and multiplies it into unrelated questions (cleaner: 39/50 answers at exactly $20/h).
  2. “Spent time to save money” memories become a personality trait. The trace calls Sam “cost-conscious” in ~95% of calls, and every answer drops, usually below the memory's own number.
  3. With several memories it picks one, it doesn't average. 0/380 answers equal the mean of the shown rates. The Disney pass ($15) and the cleaner ($20) win most contests; the wage almost never does (9/160).
  4. Contradictions are noticed but not resolved. No trace says time might be worth different amounts in different settings.
  5. Work is undervalued. Turning down a $29/h shift lowers the price Sam asks for weekend work, and being paid to give up time is priced below paying to save it.

1. Design

The prompt (one call per question)

Please read this description of yourself and answer as this person.

Persona · fixed
You are Sam, a 31-year-old who shares an apartment with your partner, Jordan, and a rescue dog named Pepper. On weekday evenings you like cooking, watching basketball, and calling your sister. On weekends you play in a recreational soccer league, see friends, and now and then take a trip. People describe you as friendly.
Memories · varied (0 to 9)
Some things you remember from your recent life:
– Last night you chose Lyft's "Wait & Save" option: you waited an extra 8 minutes to save $4.
– On your commute to work you take a toll road that costs $9 and saves you about 15 minutes…

Now please answer this survey question as yourself. There are no right or wrong answers.

Question · 1 of 10
In general, how much is one hour of your time worth to you, in dollars?

Reply with only a single dollar amount, written as a plain number with no dollar sign, words, or range.

The memory wording was chosen so every memory implies a different $/hour; a numeric answer can therefore be traced to the memory it came from (±3%). Two memories also carry non-time motives (fun, a date).

Cleaner, nonstop flight: “$60 divided by 3 hours is $20 per hour. If the nonstop flight saves 3 hours… 3 hours times…” → $60
Valet on a date, train delay: “…$210 per hour. If he values his time that high, 5 hours would be 5×210 = $1050. But that seems high…” → $1,050 (5 of 5 samples)

The valet memory is used at face value even though it happened on a date; the trace rarely treats the date as a reason to discount it.

“The key is that he's shown to be cost-conscious, so he's not going to pay a lot… the answer is $15.”

Both kinds of memory bound the same quantity, yet one is used as a price and the other as a character description.

2c. Turning down paid work is read backwards

Sam turned down a Saturday shift at $29/h, so his free Saturday is worth more than $29/h. Lowest pay for a 4-hour Saturday job: $10, $20, $30, $50, $116. Only one sample reaches 4 × $29.

“Since he turned down the higher rate, maybe he would accept something lower, like $50.”

2d. Being paid to give up time is valued below paying to save time

With no memory: weekend gig $12.5/h and research study $15/h, versus the general question $20/h and the nonstop flight $50/h. People usually show the opposite gap (they demand more to give time up).

3d. Contradictions are noticed, not resolved

Lyft (worth ≤ $30/h) and the toll (worth ≥ $36/h) can't both hold for a single hourly value. 5/20 traces with both flag it, then pick one:

“…$36 per hour… the Lyft example… that's $30. Hmm, conflicting numbers. But maybe he's more like the toll road.”

No trace suggests the standard resolution: commute time and leisure time can be worth different amounts.

3e. Other carry-overs

  • The $210/h valet rate wins only 37/340 times when other memories are present: it is outvoted, not discounted.
  • With all nine memories, the weekend gig is $80, $80 for 4 hours ($20/h, the cleaner rate), still below the $29/h shift already refused.

4. Caveats and next steps

  • One model (Qwen3-8B, 4-bit), one persona, one wording per memory; 5 samples per cell in stage 1, 2 in stage 2.
  • Floor vs. ceiling is confounded with topic: every ceiling memory is a small errand. Next: flip direction within one scenario (paid the toll vs. skipped it).
  • Easy arithmetic vs. closeness to the default: add an easy-arithmetic memory with an unusual rate (e.g. $120 for 2 hours).
  • Keyword “mentions” are uninformative: nearly every trace restates the memories, so use is measured from the dollar answers.
  • 12/500 stage-1 traces cite an “example answer” or “previous interaction” that doesn't exist.
  • No human comparison yet; the same prompts can run on Prolific.
